Understanding the Genomic Heterogeneity of North African Imazighen: From Broad to Microgeographical Perspectives

Vilà-Valls, L., Abdeli, A., Lucas-Sánchez, M., Bekada, A., Calafell, F., Benhassine, T., & Comas, D. (2024). Scientific Reports, 14, 11452.

View Full Paper (DOI) → View PubMed Record →

Summary

The Imazighen (Berbers) represent the indigenous descendants of North Africa, speaking Afro-Asiatic Amazigh languages across Morocco, Algeria, Tunisia, Libya, and Saharan regions. In this comprehensive 2024 study, Vilà-Valls and colleagues compiled the **largest Amazigh genomic dataset to date**, providing high-resolution insight into microgeographical variation and historical population dynamics.

The study reveals that while Imazighen share a common autochthonous North African genetic backbone, significant microgeographical heterogeneity exists between valleys, mountain refuges, and desert communities. The authors identified a group-wide historical admixture wave occurring around the **12th century CE**—a period corresponding with major Almoravid and Almohad empire movements and increased trans-Saharan trade network activity—as well as a secondary localized wave in the 19th century.

The research also demonstrates that long-term assortative mating was rare, showing that Amazigh communities maintained dynamic gene flow across trade routes while preserving indigenous identity.

Key Takeaways

  • Analyzed the largest Amazigh-focused genomic dataset to characterize population structure across the Maghreb.
  • Identified microgeographical genetic variation between different Amazigh groups driven by topography and effective population size.
  • Discovered a major group-wide admixture wave dated to the **12th century CE** (Almoravid/Almohad historical era).
  • Re-evaluated trans-Saharan connectivity, documenting historical interactions across medieval Saharan trade networks.
  • Showed that assortative mating was historically rare among Amazigh groups, refuting models of static isolation.

Relevance to Our Lineage

For the Souss-Massa region and the Chtouka confederation, this study illuminates how local geography (such as the Anti-Atlas mountains and Souss Valley) preserved high indigenous Berber ancestry while participating in broader 12th-century historical movements. It places Souss-Massa genetic heritage within a dynamic historical framework of resilient indigenous continuity coupled with regional Mediterranean connectivity.
